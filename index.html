Fast and Durable
Mobile Tests with Mobium

Turn off the motion: what the system switches do, what they miss, and how to prove the animation is gone — measured on four devices: two real phones, a simulator and an emulator.

Animations are for humans.

They are how a person follows what changed on a screen. A test is not a person. To a test, every animation is one of two things:

time it waits

a slow suite

a race it loses

a flaky suite

The two failure modes look different, which is why teams rarely trace them to the same cause. This is the thesis; everything after is how.

01

What motion costs a test

Before turning anything off: where the time goes, and where the flakes come from.

Section divider. Two seconds on screen. Do not read it aloud.

Three costs, and the first one is measurable

costwhat happenswhat it costs you
timethe tool waits for the target to settle~2 s a sliding target — measured
racesa tap aimed at something still moving; a read mid-transitiona flake that a retry hides
CPUthe device under test renders frames nobody watchesa busier device

Two seconds is invisible in one test. Across a suite, on every run, for everyone waiting on the build, it is the feedback loop.

Two seconds: a target that slides in over two seconds cost 3.2 s on the iPhone and 2.5 s on Android when the tool waited for it, against 1.3 s and well under one second with the motion off. The race cost does not show up as time — it shows up as a retry.

Two kinds of animation. Different switches.

The system's

Transitions between screens and apps, drawn by the OS.

A device setting turns them off.

The app's own

Anything the app animates itself: slide-ins, bounces, confetti.

Only the app can turn them off.

This distinction carries the rest of the talk. Part 2 is the left box, Part 3 the right one — and Part 3 is where the time actually goes.

02

The system's animations

Android: three settings. iOS: one switch, and only Settings can flip it.

Section divider. Two seconds on screen. Do not read it aloud.

Android: three scales to zero. No root, emulator or phone.

$ adb shell settings put global window_animation_scale 0

$ adb shell settings put global transition_animation_scale 0

$ adb shell settings put global animator_duration_scale 0

$ adb shell settings get global animator_duration_scale

0

Put them back after, as you found them — including “not set at all”, which is how an untouched device reads. On a phone, these belong to somebody.

An untouched emulator reads animator_duration_scale as null, not 1. Restoring it as 1 leaves the device different from how you found it; delete the key instead.

iOS: no command. Reduce Motion, in Settings.

Settings › Accessibility › Motion › Reduce Motion

Nothing outside the phone can set it. A test that wants it has to drive the Settings app — about 25 seconds a change on a real iPhone, measured.

So: once per device, in setup. Never per test.

Reduce Motion reduces; it does not remove. Settings' own push still slides with it on. Its real power is what it tells the app — which is Part 3.

With Mobium

One call, both platforms, read back, put back.

mobium accessibility reduce_motion on

The three scales on Android; a trip through Settings on an iPhone. Confirmed by reading the setting back, and returned to what it was when the session ends. Read it afterwards anyway: once, on the iPhone, it was not — the cause is still open.

A setting changed on somebody's phone and never changed back is a defect, whoever wrote the test. So the demo's recordings read it before and after.

Droppable if tight: its point returns on the habits card. The restore matters most on a real phone — the iPhone in the demo is the speaker's own. On 2026-10-04 one test run left its Reduce Motion off when it had been on; it was put back by hand, two reruns restored correctly, and the cause is an open lead in Mobium's roadmap. Say it: it is the talk's own rule applied to the tool.

03

The app's own animations

Where the time actually goes — and the switch from Part 2 does not reach it on its own.

Section divider. Two seconds on screen. Do not read it aloud.

All three scales at zero. The app's animation, timed by the app:

targetscales at 1scales at 0
ignores the setting2533 ms2517 ms
honors the setting2601 ms767 ms

Every setting read back 0. The animation on the app's own clock slid for two and a half seconds anyway. The one that asked “should motion be reduced?” appeared at once.

Android 15 emulator, MobiumApp's Motion Demo  ·  demo/animation-scales.sh

Two identical animations, one line apart: one reads the platform's reduce-motion answer, one does not. The zeroed scales are how Android answers yes. So the system switch turns off the app's animations only when the app listens.

Two ways to stop the app's own motion

A test build

Every duration forced to zero behind a test-only flag.

You test a build nobody ships, through a flag nothing tests.

Honor Reduce Motion

The app skips motion when the user asked for less.

Part 2's switch now reaches it — in the build you ship, on a path real users take.

Recommend the right-hand one, and say why twice: the fast path becomes a real path, and it is an accessibility fix the app owes its users anyway. The test build is the fallback when the app team cannot change the code.

The one line, per framework

frameworkask whether motion is reduced
UIKitUIAccessibility.isReduceMotionEnabled
SwiftUI@Environment(\.accessibilityReduceMotion)
Android viewsValueAnimator.areAnimatorsEnabled()
React NativeAccessibilityInfo.isReduceMotionEnabled()
Web / WebView@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)

Then animate with a duration of zero, or set the final state directly. Listen for changes — the setting can flip while the app runs.

React Native on Android answers from the transition scale alone — measured: animator scale 0 by itself left it false. Zero all three and every reader agrees.

The rule

Make the fast path a real path.

Turn motion off through the switch users have, and make the app listen to it.

The speed-up and the accessibility fix are the same line of code — and the test now exercises something users actually get.

A test-only shortcut is a second app, and nobody tests that one.

This is the line people should leave with if they leave with one.

04

Proving it, and what stays

A setting that reads 0 is not an animation that stopped.

Section divider. Two seconds on screen. Do not read it aloud.

The iPhone's on-time here is 2.9 s against 3.2 s on the previous slide: the procedure differs (here Replay follows straight after the screen opens), and why that is worth 0.3 s was not established — compare within a table, not across.

Some motion never stops. Wait for stillness — or refuse.

$ mobium tap @e2   # a falling piece of confetti

error: failed check stable: it is still moving after 5s —

it was at [297,1260][315,1293] and is now at [306,1530][327,1560]

$ echo $?

6   # timeout

Spinners, confetti, live content. Mobium waits for every target to stop moving before it acts — and a target that never holds still is a failure with a code, not something to chase.

Chasing a moving target is how a race becomes a flake: sometimes the tap lands, sometimes it does not, and the retry hides it. A refusal fails the same way every time.

Four habits for fast, durable mobile tests

01

Turn off the system's animations once per device, and put them back as you found them.

02

Make the app honor Reduce Motion. The fast path becomes a real path, in the build you ship.

03

Time it with the app, and keep a control. A setting that reads 0 is not an animation that stopped.

04

Wait for stillness; refuse what never stops. A chased target is a flake waiting to happen.

The takeaway card — this is the slide people screenshot

Do not elaborate. Read each one once and move to the close. If you are over time, this is the slide to land on and stop.

Fast  ·  durable  ·  measured

Turn off every animation you can — the system's with a setting, the app's by having it listen — and wait out the rest.

You get time back, and a whole class of races leaves your suite. How much depends on your app and how much it moves. But every second you take off a test comes off the feedback loop of everyone who waits on your build.
